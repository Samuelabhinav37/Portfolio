Mail the key and a courier copies it in transit. Don't mail it and your friend can never lock the box in the first place. For most of human history, this key distribution problem meant secure communication needed a physical meeting or a courier you already trusted, before a single secret ever moved.

In 1976 and 1977, that assumption finally broke. Whitfield Diffie and Martin Hellman had already shown two strangers a way to agree on a shared secret across an open wire. A year later, Ron Rivest, Adi Shamir, and Leonard Adleman, working at MIT, pushed the same asymmetric idea further still: a key pair where the key that locks a message is mathematically incapable of unlocking it again.

<Mnote sub="classified for 24 years" />The idea had actually surfaced three years earlier and stayed buried. British cryptographer Clifford Cocks solved the same problem inside GCHQ in 1973, but his work sat classified until 1997. Rivest, Shamir, and Adleman's 1977 paper is the version the world actually got to build on.

Anyone walking past can drop a letter through the slot. Only the carrier holding the physical key to the back door can ever retrieve it. Once a letter drops through the slot, even the person who dropped it can't reach back in and pull it out. Locking and unlocking are two genuinely separate operations now, not the same action run twice.

## Two keys, two jobs

- **Public key**: published openly, on GitHub, key servers, DNS records, certificates. Encrypts a message meant for you, or verifies a signature you claim to have made.
- **Private key**: guarded fiercely, often inside a hardware enclave or a TPM chip. Decrypts whatever arrives encrypted to you, or creates a signature only you could have produced.

That split covers two genuinely different jobs:

1. **Confidentiality**: Alice looks up your public key, encrypts a note with it, and only your private key opens it back up. Not even Alice can reverse what she just encrypted.
2. **Digital signatures**: you encrypt a document's hash with your private key. Anyone holding your public key can decrypt that hash and check it against the document, proving you wrote it and that nobody altered it afterward.

## Math behind the curtain: one-way traps

Two keys stay mathematically linked without either one giving the other away because asymmetric cryptography leans on trapdoor one-way functions: problems easy to compute in one direction and effectively impossible to reverse without a specific piece of secret information.

RSA leans on how hard factoring a huge number back into its original primes is. Elliptic Curve Cryptography reaches the same guarantee through a different one-way trap, the algebra of points along a curve, and gets there with far smaller keys.

<DetectionOpportunity>A public key alone only proves someone holds the matching private key. It says nothing about whose hands that private key is actually in, which is exactly the gap a certificate and a Certificate Authority exist to close. Never trust a bare key. Trust a signed one.</DetectionOpportunity>

> Two keys did what one key never could: let the lock travel anywhere while the key that opens it goes nowhere.

## Modern takeaway

Asymmetric math is thousands of times heavier than symmetric encryption, so modern systems only use it where it earns its cost:

1. **Asymmetric encryption** (RSA or ECC) runs for roughly the first fifty milliseconds of a connection, just long enough to exchange a temporary key.
2. **Symmetric encryption** (AES-GCM) takes over for every file, video stream, and byte of bulk web traffic after that.
